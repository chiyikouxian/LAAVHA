%% Abstract (approx 200 words, self-contained)
\begin{abstract}
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) operating in heterogeneous wireless networks require seamless connectivity across multiple radio access technologies, including fifth-generation (5G), Long-Term Evolution (LTE), and Wi-Fi. During aerial missions, UAVs frequently traverse coverage boundaries among these networks, creating vertical-handover (VHO) challenges involving decision latency, ping-pong handovers, and fixed attribute weights that cannot adapt to dynamic channel conditions and flight phases. This study proposes a set of decision-level network selection algorithms to optimize the handover strategy, including the long short-term memory (LSTM)-attention-based adaptive vertical handover algorithm (LAAVHA), adaptive dynamic hysteresis (ADH), and a risk-sensitive technique for order preference by similarity to the ideal solution (RS-TOPSIS), which collectively form the attention-LSTM enhanced risk-aware vertical handover algorithm (ALERA). LAAVHA integrates stacked-LSTM state prediction, attention-based dynamic weighting, improved TOPSIS ranking, and dual-hysteresis decision-making. ADH adapts hysteresis parameters to flight speed and serving-network channel fluctuations, while RS-TOPSIS penalizes volatile closeness scores to improve candidate ranking. Simulation results demonstrate reduced unnecessary handovers, improved decision efficiency, sustained remote-sensing data continuity, and greater robustness to abrupt channel variations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are increasingly deployed in heterogeneous wireless network environments, where multiple radio access technologies—including fifth-generation (5G), Long-Term Evolution (LTE), and Wi-Fi—coexist with overlapping coverage areas. UAV mobility introduces unique vertical handover (VHO) challenges that differ fundamentally from ground-based scenarios: rapid altitude changes, high-speed boundary crossings, and three-dimensional trajectory variations create dynamic channel conditions that demand intelligent network selection strategies. The core technical challenge lies in managing seamless connectivity across coverage boundaries while maintaining quality of service for real-time aerial data transmission \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3}. This study addresses the network selection decision problem: determining when to initiate a handover and which target network to select, based on predicted channel conditions and dynamic attribute weighting. Existing VHO methods mainly employ multiple attribute decision making (MADM) \cite{ref7}, including the technique for order preference by similarity to the ideal solution (TOPSIS) \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10}, VIsekriterijumska Optimizacija I Kompromisno Resenje (VIKOR) \cite{ref11}, and the Grey relational analysis (GRA), complex proportional assessment (COPRAS), stable preference ordering toward ideal solution (SPOTIS), and simple additive weighting (SAW) methods \cite{ref12}. Beyond deterministic MADM frameworks, fuzzy-logic-based approaches such as Fuzzy-VHO apply fuzzy inference to handle uncertain channel measurements and handover decisions \cite{ref4}. More recently, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods have been explored to learn adaptive handover policies from experience \cite{ref16,ref17,ref18}. Despite their different formulations, these approaches have common limitations. MADM and fuzzy methods typically use fixed weights—often derived from entropy weighting or the analytic hierarchy process \cite{ref12,ref13,ref14,ref15}—limiting adaptation to varying flight phases and channel conditions, and rely on current network indicators without predicting future states, potentially delaying handovers until link failure when a UAV approaches coverage limits \cite{ref16,ref17}. DRL-based methods remain limited by black-box decision-making and unstable training, which undermine interpretability and reliability in safety-critical aerial missions.

LSTM networks alleviate vanishing gradients through gated structures \cite{ref19} and capture long-range temporal dependencies; they have accordingly been used to forecast handover-related network states in 5G heterogeneous networks \cite{ref20,ref21,ref22}. Because such forecasting is trained by supervised regression against measured next-period states, it can be inserted into a multi-attribute decision framework as an explicit prediction stage, without replacing the decision rule by a learned policy. In existing schemes, however, the forecast serves mainly to refresh the indicator values that enter the decision layer, whereas the weights and thresholds of that layer are still specified offline; the benefit is therefore confined to smoother inputs rather than adaptive decision logic.

VHO in UAV heterogeneous wireless networks involves two challenges. The first is informational. Conventional MADM ranks candidates on instantaneous indicators and therefore cannot anticipate a link that is about to fail, and although a single LSTM can forecast the next-period state, existing designs use that forecast only to refresh the indicators while leaving the decision rule untouched: the forecast changes what is known, not how the decision is made. The second is parametric. Attribute weights are fixed using entropy weighting or the analytic hierarchy process \cite{ref12,ref13,ref14,ref15}, and the score margin and confirmation window are held constant, yet the relative importance of SINR, delay, throughput, and packet loss differs between cruise and imaging phases, and the channel is quiescent in steady flight but fluctuates sharply near coverage boundaries and obstacles. A margin lax enough to avoid chattering in steady flight responds to boundary noise, while one strict enough to suppress that noise delays necessary handovers, and the same tension governs the length of the confirmation window. Handover instability therefore stems less from limited sensing than from decision logic whose parameters cannot follow the flight state. Addressing these challenges requires a framework that integrates temporal prediction with adaptive decision parameters.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes LAAVHA, which integrates LSTM prediction, attention-based dynamic weighting, improved TOPSIS with fused states, and dual hysteresis. The stacked LSTM addresses the informational challenge by predicting short-term network-state changes; attention-based weighting addresses the parametric challenge by adapting attribute importance to flight speed and altitude; improved TOPSIS fuses current and predicted states to enable proactive decisions. LAAVHA is further enhanced with ADH and RS-TOPSIS to form ALERA. ADH adapts the handover threshold and confirmation window to serving-network SINR variation and flight speed, addressing the fixed-parameter limitation under varying channel conditions. RS-TOPSIS penalizes historically volatile candidates to prevent false handovers caused by transient score peaks.

Simulation results across 250 extended 50-second trajectories show that LAAVHA achieves the lowest mean handover count ($2.13$) among 11 algorithms, reducing handovers by $46.0\%-94.8\%$ versus eight baselines. End-to-end QoS evaluation across 50 representative scenarios demonstrates that LAAVHA simultaneously achieves the highest mean throughput ($2.00$ Mbps) and lowest mean delay ($1.00$ ms) among evaluated algorithms. Ablation experiments using 250 seeds with 50-second missions show that removing the LSTM prediction module increases the mean count to $5.55$, while removing attention-based dynamic weighting increases it to $24.25$, confirming their complementary roles in handover stability. ALERA completes all necessary handovers during sustained link degradation while producing no false handovers under transient fluctuations, whereas a fixed low-threshold scheme causes two additional ping-pong handovers. These results demonstrate that ADH and RS-TOPSIS enhance robustness under channel fluctuations without suppressing necessary handovers.

\section{Network Scenario and Network-State Parameters}\label{sec:scenario}

A UAV aerial mission is considered over a $2000~\mathrm{m}\times2000~\mathrm{m}$ heterogeneous wireless network coverage area, with a UAV following a predefined route at $50-150~\mathrm{m}$ altitude and $5-30~\mathrm{m/s}$ cruise speed. Wi-Fi, LTE, and 5G serve as candidate networks with different coverage ranges and deployment densities. Wi-Fi hotspots have an approximate $200~\mathrm{m}$ radius and are placed near ground control stations and key operational sites. LTE base stations provide $1-2~\mathrm{km}$ coverage and are sparsely deployed along the route. 5G base stations, with an approximate $500~\mathrm{m}$ radius, are concentrated in the eastern data-aggregation area, offering high-rate access but incomplete coverage continuity. The UAV periodically collects the state of each candidate network for handover decisions \cite{ref23}.

\caption{Heterogeneous wireless network coverage and UAV trajectory in the mission area.}
\label{fig:network_coverage}

Wi-Fi, LTE, and 5G provide overlapping coverage at different scales (Figure~\ref{fig:network_coverage}). Wi-Fi hotspots mainly serve ground stations and key imaging sites; LTE base stations offer relatively continuous wide-area connectivity; and 5G base stations provide high-rate transmission within the eastern data-aggregation area but with limited coverage continuity. The 5G next generation Node B (gNB) provides 5G coverage, radio-resource scheduling, and high-rate data transmission. As the UAV follows its route, it traverses overlapping coverage areas and requires VHO when the serving network degrades; another candidate offers better overall performance. The coverage scales correspond to the specified radii and define the basis for subsequent network-state collection and candidate evaluation.

For candidate network $i$, the state vector at time $t$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
S_i(t)=\left[\mathrm{SINR}_i(t),\,\mathrm{RSRP}_i(t),\,D_i(t),\,T_i(t),\,\mathrm{PLR}_i(t)\right],
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{SINR}_i(t)$, $\mathrm{RSRP}_i(t)$, $D_i(t)$, $T_i(t)$, and $\mathrm{PLR}_i(t)$ denote the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), reference signal received power (RSRP), end-to-end delay, throughput, and packet loss ratio (PLR) of candidate \textit{i} at time \textit{t}, respectively. These five indicators characterize candidate-network performance from complementary perspectives. As the UAV moves across coverage boundaries, $\mathrm{SINR}_i(t)$ typically declines from tens of decibels toward the noise floor, reflecting signal degradation caused by distance and obstruction and providing a direct handover cue. $\mathrm{RSRP}_i(t)$, together with $\mathrm{SINR}_i(t)$, determines whether the link budget meets the demodulation threshold; below receiver sensitivity, reliable physical-layer connectivity cannot be established even with acceptable SINR. The delay $D_i(t)$ represents the round-trip time of a packet between the ground station and UAV application layer, affecting responsiveness and safety margins during high-speed cruise. Throughput $T_i(t)$ measures deliverable payload bits per unit time; insufficient throughput prevents time-sensitive data payloads from being transmitted during a single overflight. While $\mathrm{PLR}_i(t)$ reflects transmission reliability, even with adequate signal strength and throughput, high $\mathrm{PLR}_i(t)$ can cause data loss or quality degradation. $\mathrm{SINR}_i(t)$, $\mathrm{RSRP}_i(t)$, and $T_i(t)$ are benefit criteria, whereas $D_i(t)$ and $\mathrm{PLR}_i(t)$ are cost criteria. To enable unified multi-attribute evaluation across indicators with different units and ranges, min-max normalization maps all raw measurements to $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{s-s_{\min}}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}},\quad \text{for each indicator }j,
\label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
where $s$ denotes the raw measurement, and $s_{\min}$ and $s_{\max}$ represent the minimum and maximum values observed across the three candidates at the current decision period. After normalization, larger values consistently indicate better performance, directly supporting benefit criteria (SINR, RSRP, throughput) while requiring subsequent inversion for cost criteria (delay, PLR) in the decision stage \cite{ref11}.

Equations~\eqref{eq:state} and \eqref{eq:norm} together define the normalized state representation used throughout the algorithm. Stacking $S_i(t)$ for the three candidates forms the current-state matrix $S_{\mathrm{cur}}\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}$ for attention-based weight generation (Section~\ref{sec:weight}), while stacking one candidate's states over 10 consecutive periods forms the historical tensor $X_k\in\mathbf{R}^{10\times5}$ for LSTM prediction (Section~\ref{sec:pred}). These representations capture both cross-candidate comparison and per-candidate temporal evolution, providing the foundation for the adaptive handover framework in Section~\ref{sec:laavha}.

\section{LAAVHA Adaptive Vertical Handover Algorithm}\label{sec:laavha}

Based on the network scenario and network-state parameters (Section~\ref{sec:scenario}), this section proposes LAAVHA, an adaptive VHO algorithm that integrates LSTM and an attention mechanism. It overcomes the fixed weighting and lack of temporal prediction in conventional MADM methods for UAV aerial missions in heterogeneous wireless networks. Using the five-dimensional state vector and sliding window (Section~\ref{sec:scenario}), LAAVHA performs stacked-LSTM prediction, attention-based dynamic weighting, and improved TOPSIS decision-making with predicted states. ADH and RS-TOPSIS are then incorporated to form ALERA.

\centering

(a) LAAVHA algorithm framework\\[3mm]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/fig_alera_framework_en.png}\\[1mm]
(b) ALERA algorithm framework
\caption{LAAVHA and ALERA algorithm frameworks.}
\label{fig:frameworks}
\end{figure}

LAAVHA comprises network-state prediction, dynamic-weight generation, candidate ranking, and dual-hysteresis decision-making, with ADH and RS-TOPSIS extending the decision chain (Figure~\ref{fig:frameworks}). These modules form a sequential flow around a common network state. The historical sequence $X_k$, collected and normalized in Section~\ref{sec:scenario}, enters the stacked LSTM to produce the next-period predicted state $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$. Additionally, the current-state matrix $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ enters the attention module with mobility features, including speed and altitude, to generate dynamic weight $w$. Then $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$, and $w$ are combined in improved TOPSIS to obtain candidate closeness score $C_i$. Finally, $C_i$ and the current serving network $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$ enter the hysteresis decision module with ALERA enhancements to determine the final handover. Therefore, Section~\ref{sec:pred} predicts future states, Section~\ref{sec:weight} determines attribute importance, Section~\ref{sec:topsis} ranks candidates using current and future states, and Section~\ref{sec:alera} determines whether handover is appropriate under channel fluctuations.

\subsection{Network-State Prediction using a Stacked LSTM}\label{sec:pred}

The time-window length is set to $T_s=10$, covering 10 consecutive decision periods over $1.0~\mathrm{s}$ with a decision interval of $\tau=0.1~\mathrm{s}$. Candidate networks $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ represent 5G, LTE, and Wi-Fi, respectively. The historical input tensor $X_k\in\mathbf{R}^{10\times5}$ is constructed by row-wise stacking the normalized five-dimensional state vectors across the 10 periods. The prediction target is the next-period state vector $\hat{s}_k(t+\tau)\in\mathbf{R}^5$:
\begin{align}
h_{1,k} &= \mathrm{LSTM}_1(X_k;\theta_1), && h_{1,k}\in\mathbf{R}^{10\times128}, \label{eq:lstm1}\\
h_{2,k} &= \mathrm{LSTM}_2(h_{1,k};\theta_2), && h_{2,k}\in\mathbf{R}^{10\times64}, \label{eq:lstm2}\\
\hat{s}_k(t+\tau) &= W_{\mathrm{pred}}h_{2,k}[-1]+b_{\mathrm{pred}}, && \hat{s}_k\in\mathbf{R}^5. \label{eq:lstm3}
\end{align}
where $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ denote the trainable parameters of the two LSTM layers, including the weights and biases of the input, forget, and output gates and cell states, while $W_{\mathrm{pred}}\in\mathbf{R}^{5\times64}$ and $b_{\mathrm{pred}}\in\mathbf{R}^5$ represent the fully connected prediction-head weight and bias, respectively, and $h_{2,k}[-1]$ indicates the final output of $\mathrm{LSTM}_2$. Equations~\eqref{eq:lstm1}--\eqref{eq:lstm3} define the prediction pipeline: 10 steps $\times$ 5 indicators $\rightarrow\mathrm{LSTM}_1(5\rightarrow128)\rightarrow\mathrm{LSTM}_2(128\rightarrow64)\rightarrow\mathrm{fc}_{\mathrm{pred}}(64\rightarrow5)$. The three candidate networks share $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $W_{\mathrm{pred}}$, and $b_{\mathrm{pred}}$, while using their respective historical sequences $X_k$. The resulting predictions are stacked into $S_{\mathrm{pred}}\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}$, representing the next-period values of the five indicators for each network. The model is trained offline using supervised learning with mean squared error (MSE) loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|S_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(n)} - S_{\mathrm{target}}^{(n)}\right\|_2^2,
\label{eq:loss_pred}
\end{equation}
where $n=1,\ldots,N$ indexes training samples, each comprising a historical sequence $X_k^{(n)}$ and the corresponding ground-truth next-period state $S_{\mathrm{target}}^{(n)}$. Training hyperparameters are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:platform}. The predicted state $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is subsequently fused with the current state $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ in the TOPSIS decision stage (Section~\ref{sec:topsis}), enabling forward-looking candidate evaluation.

\subsection{Dynamic Weight Generation for Flight Phases}\label{sec:weight}

Network-attribute importance varies with the VHO decisions. During cruise, link stability and flight-control delay are more critical to safety, whereas during image return at imaging sites, throughput and packet loss become more important for timely and reliable transmission of high-resolution images \cite{ref24}. Fixed weights cannot capture these changing priorities.

To capture phase-dependent requirements, LAAVHA employs self-attention \cite{ref26,ref27}. The five-dimensional state vectors of the three candidates are row-wise stacked into $S_{\mathrm{cur}}\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}$, with $Q=K=V=S_{\mathrm{cur}}$, \texttt{embed\_dim} = 5, and \texttt{num\_heads} = 1. The \texttt{embed\_dim} denotes the feature dimension of each attention input and is set to 5, corresponding to the normalized SINR, RSRP, delay, throughput, and packet loss ratio indicators, and \texttt{num\_heads} represents the number of parallel attention heads and is set to 1. With five attributes, multiple heads would provide too few features per head and reduce weight interpretability. A single head therefore captures attribute correlations directly. The self-attention mechanism captures cross-network dependencies among the candidate networks, where the attention matrix quantifies how each network's state should be aggregated based on inter-network relationships. Cross-network mean pooling then extracts the attribute-level context $c\in\mathbf{R}^5$ from the attention output. The resulting representations are combined with UAV mobility features to generate dynamic attribute weights for subsequent TOPSIS-based network selection. Flight speed $v$ and altitude $h$ are mapped by $W_{\mathrm{mob}}\in\mathbf{R}^{16\times2}$ to mobility feature $m\in\mathbf{R}^{16}$. The concatenated results are projected by a fully connected layer and normalized by softmax to obtain the five-dimensional dynamic weight $w$:
\begin{align}
Q &= K = V = S_{\mathrm{cur}}\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}, \label{eq:attn1}\\
A &= \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{5}}\right)V,\quad A\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}, \label{eq:attn2}\\
c &= \mathrm{mean}(A,\mathrm{dim}=0)\in\mathbf{R}^5, \label{eq:attn3}\\
m &= \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(W_{\mathrm{mob}}[v,h]^\top\right),\quad m\in\mathbf{R}^{16}, \label{eq:attn4}\\
w &= \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(W_{\mathrm{weight}}[c\parallel m]\right),\quad W_{\mathrm{weight}}:\mathbf{R}^{21}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{5},\quad w\in\mathbf{R}^{5},\quad \sum_j w_j=1. \label{eq:attn5}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:attn1}--\eqref{eq:attn5} define the dynamic-weight procedure: $S_{\mathrm{cur}}\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}$ enters self-attention, producing the $3\times5$ matrix $A$; cross-network mean pooling yields the five-dimensional attribute context $c$; speed and altitude are mapped to the 16-dimensional mobility feature $m$; and their 21-dimensional concatenation is projected by $W_{\mathrm{weight}}$ and softmax-normalized to obtain the five-dimensional dynamic weight $w$. The dynamic weight $w$ is subsequently applied in the TOPSIS ranking stage (Section~\ref{sec:topsis}), enabling SINR, RSRP, delay, throughput, and packet loss ratio to contribute adaptively to the final closeness score $C_i$ while the LSTM branch predicts future state changes.

\subsection{Improved TOPSIS Decision Making with Fused Predicted States}\label{sec:topsis}

Since aerial mission performance depends jointly on signal strength, transmission capability, and reliability, no single indicator defines the optimal network. TOPSIS ranks candidates by their proximity to the virtual ideal and negative-ideal networks \cite{ref8,ref9}. To incorporate future conditions, LAAVHA fuses the current state $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and predicted state $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$ to form the decision matrix:
\begin{align}
d_{ij} &= \alpha s_{\mathrm{cur}}(i,j)+(1-\alpha)s_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,j),\quad \alpha=0.6, \label{eq:fuse}\\
d_{ij} &\leftarrow 1-d_{ij},\quad j\in\{\mathrm{delay},\mathrm{PLR}\}. \label{eq:cost}
\end{align}
where $\alpha=0.6$ (justified in Section~\ref{sec:platform}) balances current reliability with predictive foresight, and Equation~\eqref{eq:cost} inverts delay and PLR so all attributes follow larger-is-better orientation. The resulting decision matrix $D\in\mathbf{R}^{3\times5}$ enters standard TOPSIS:
\begin{align}
r_{ij} &= \frac{d_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}d_{kj}^{2}}}, \label{eq:topsis1}\\
v_{ij} &= w_j r_{ij}, \label{eq:topsis2}\\
v_j^+ &= \max_i v_{ij},\quad v_j^-=\min_i v_{ij}, \label{eq:topsis3}\\
D_i^+ &= \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{5}(v_{ij}-v_j^+)^2},\quad
D_i^-=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{5}(v_{ij}-v_j^-)^2}, \label{eq:topsis4}\\
C_i &= \frac{D_i^-}{D_i^+ + D_i^-},\quad C_i\in[0,1]. \label{eq:topsis5}
\end{align}
where $r_{ij}$ denotes the vector-normalized element of fused matrix $D$, and $\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}d_{kj}^{2}}$ represents the Euclidean norm of attribute $j$ across the three candidates, with $k$ as the summation index, $w_j$ indicates the dynamic weight of attribute $j$ from the attention module, $v_{ij}$ signifies the weighted normalized element, $v_j^+$ and $v_j^-$ correspond to the positive and negative ideal solutions, defined as the largest and smallest weighted normalized values for attribute $j$, respectively, $D_i^+$ and $D_i^-$ are the distances of candidate $i$ from these solutions, and $C_i\in[0,1]$ is its relative closeness. Therefore, Equations~\eqref{eq:topsis1}--\eqref{eq:topsis5} convert the $3\times5$ fused matrix into three closeness scores through normalization, dynamic weighting, ideal-solution determination, and Euclidean-distance calculation. A value closer to 1 indicates stronger overall support for image transmission, flight-control responsiveness, and link reliability.
\begin{align}
N^* &= \arg\max_i C_i, \label{eq:select}\\
\mathrm{Decision} &=
\begin{cases}
\text{switch to }N^*, & \min_{l=0}^{T_h-1}\left[C_t(N^*)-C_{t-l}(N_{\mathrm{cur}})\right]>\Delta_{\mathrm{th}},\\
\text{keep }N_{\mathrm{cur}}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases} \label{eq:hysteresis}
\end{align}
where $N^*$ denotes the candidate with the highest current closeness, $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$ represents the serving network, $C_t(\cdot)$ indicates the closeness score at decision period $t$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.05$ specifies the required score advantage, and $T_h=3$ specifies the confirmation window (justified in Section~\ref{sec:platform}). The min operator enforces that the advantage must persist across all of the latest $T_h$ periods, suppressing transient-fluctuation-induced ping-pong handovers.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:laavha} implements the three-stage LAAVHA procedure. Stage 1 predicts $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$ via stacked LSTM and generates dynamic weight $w$ via attention. Stage 2 fuses $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$ with cost-attribute inversion, applies dynamic weighting, and ranks candidates by TOPSIS closeness $C_i$. Stage 3 triggers handover only when the advantage persists for $T_h$ consecutive periods, suppressing transient-fluctuation-induced ping-pong handovers.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{LAAVHA vertical-handover decision procedure}
\label{alg:laavha}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Historical states $\{X_k\}_{k=1}^{3}$, current state $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$, mobility state $[v,h]$, serving network $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, previous confirmed candidate $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, and its consecutive count $q$
\Ensure Handover decision $\mathrm{Decision}$ and updated $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, and $q$
\State \textbf{Stage 0: Initialization}
\State Read the current decision-period state and previous hysteresis state $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, $q$
\State \textbf{Stage 1: State prediction and dynamic-weight generation}
\For{$k\gets1$ \textbf{to} $3$}
 \State $h_{1,k}\gets\mathrm{LSTM}_1(X_k;\theta_1)$, $h_{2,k}\gets\mathrm{LSTM}_2(h_{1,k};\theta_2)$
 \State $s_{\mathrm{pred},k}\gets W_{\mathrm{pred}}h_{2,k}[-1]+b_{\mathrm{pred}}$
\EndFor
\State Stack $\{s_{\mathrm{pred},k}\}_{k=1}^{3}$ as $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$
\State $A\gets\mathrm{Attention}(S_{\mathrm{cur}},S_{\mathrm{cur}},S_{\mathrm{cur}})$, $c\gets\mathrm{mean}(A,\mathrm{dim}=0)$
\State $m\gets\mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\mathrm{mob}}[v,h]^\top)$, $w\gets\mathrm{softmax}(W_{\mathrm{weight}}[c\parallel m])$
\State \textbf{Stage 2: Fused-TOPSIS candidate ranking}
\For{$i\gets1$ \textbf{to} $3$}
 \For{$j\gets1$ \textbf{to} $5$}
 \State $d_{ij}\gets0.6s_{\mathrm{cur}}(i,j)+0.4s_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,j)$
 \If{$j\in\{\mathrm{delay},\mathrm{PLR}\}$} $d_{ij}\gets1-d_{ij}$ \EndIf
 \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Compute $r_{ij}$, $v_{ij}$, $v_j^+$, $v_j^-$, $D_i^+$, $D_i^-$, and $C_i$ using Equations~\eqref{eq:topsis1}--\eqref{eq:topsis5}
\State $N^*\gets\arg\max_i C_i$
\State \textbf{Stage 3: Dual-hysteresis handover decision}
\If{$N^*\ne N_{\mathrm{cand}}$}
 \State $N_{\mathrm{cand}}\gets N^*$, $q\gets0$
\EndIf
\If{$C(N^*)-C(N_{\mathrm{cur}})>\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$}
 \State $q\gets q+1$
\Else
 \State $q\gets0$
\EndIf
\If{$q\geq T_h$}
 \State $\mathrm{Decision}\gets\text{switch to }N^*$, $N_{\mathrm{cur}}\gets N^*$
\Else
 \State $\mathrm{Decision}\gets\text{keep }N_{\mathrm{cur}}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\mathrm{Decision}$, $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, $q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{ALERA Enhanced Risk-Aware Vertical Handover Algorithm}\label{sec:alera}

The fixed $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $T_h$ in Section~\ref{sec:topsis} cannot adapt to varying channel conditions: during coverage-boundary crossings or obstructions, conservative thresholds delay necessary handovers, while low thresholds cause ping-pong handovers under transient fluctuations at high speed. Standard TOPSIS further ranks candidates solely by instantaneous closeness $C_i$, making decisions vulnerable to candidates with high but volatile scores. ADH addresses the first limitation by adjusting $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $T_h$ from serving-network SINR variation and flight speed, while RS-TOPSIS addresses the second by penalizing closeness-score instability to derive $C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}$. Together they extend LAAVHA into ALERA.

\subsubsection{Adaptive Hysteresis Parameters}\label{sec:adh}

The fixed $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.05$ and $T_h=3$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:hysteresis}) apply the same handover barrier across all channel conditions and flight phases. Under stable channels at low speed, such conservatism effectively suppresses ping-pong; however, during coverage-boundary crossings or obstructions, it delays necessary handovers, increasing link-interruption risk. Conversely, at high speed, the same window permits excessive spatial displacement before confirmation completes, reducing confirmation effectiveness. Adaptive hysteresis margins and trigger windows have been shown to improve handover decisions in heterogeneous and ultra-dense networks \cite{ref28,ref29,ref30,ref31}. Accordingly, ADH replaces fixed parameters with context-aware values derived from serving-network SINR variation and flight speed. Let $\sigma$ denote the standard deviation of serving network SINR over the latest five decision periods, and $v$ denote real-time flight speed. Both are already available: $\sigma$ is derived from the SINR history in Equation~\eqref{eq:state}, and $v$ is the mobility input used for dynamic weighting in Section~\ref{sec:weight}. The adaptive threshold and window are:
\begin{align}
\Delta_{\mathrm{th}} &= 0.03+0.05\left(\frac{\sigma}{10}\right),\quad \sigma\in[0,10], \label{eq:adh1}\\
T_h &= \max\left(2,\left\lfloor4-\frac{2v}{30}\right\rfloor\right),\quad v\in[0,30]~\mathrm{m/s}. \label{eq:adh2}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:adh1} sets a base threshold of 0.03 under stable channels ($\sigma\approx0$), allowing faster response to sustained degradation, and increases linearly to 0.08 under severe variation ($\sigma\approx10$), strengthening the handover barrier. Equation~\eqref{eq:adh2} decreases the confirmation window from 4 periods at low speed to 2 at high speed, balancing confirmation duration against position change: at $30~\mathrm{m/s}$, $T_h=4$ permits $12~\mathrm{m}$ of movement before confirmation completes, while $T_h=2$ limits this to $6~\mathrm{m}$, better supporting timely boundary handover.

\subsubsection{Risk-Sensitive TOPSIS}\label{sec:rstopsis}

Original TOPSIS ranks candidates by current closeness $C_i$, obtained from Equation~\eqref{eq:topsis5} by fusing $S_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $S_{\mathrm{pred}}$, and $w$. RS-TOPSIS retains this prediction and weighting process while adding a risk constraint to the $C_i$ series. Based on the lower-confidence-bound (LCB) principle, a historical-variation penalty is subtracted from each candidate score:
\begin{equation}
C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}=C_i-\lambda\sigma_i^C,\quad \lambda=0.5,
\label{eq:rstopsis}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_i^C$ denotes the standard deviation of the latest five scores for network $i$, and $\lambda=0.5$ is the risk-aversion coefficient ($\lambda$ setting justified in Section~\ref{sec:platform}). When current scores are similar, the mechanism favors the more stable candidate over one with a slightly higher mean but greater variation. For example, if LTE closeness increases steadily with low variance while Wi-Fi closeness fluctuates sharply, $C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}$ favors LTE even with slightly higher current Wi-Fi closeness, improving service continuity.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:alera} enhances the base closeness score at each decision period. Its $C_i$ input comes from Algorithm~\ref{alg:laavha}'s TOPSIS steps. RS-TOPSIS first computes the risk-penalized ranking, ADH then determines adaptive thresholds, and the dual-hysteresis mechanism finally decides based on persistent robust-score advantage.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{ALERA risk-aware vertical-handover enhancement procedure}
\label{alg:alera}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Base closeness $\{C_i\}_{i=1}^{3}$ and its latest $K_c=5$ history, latest five serving-network SINR values, flight speed $v$, serving network $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, previous confirmed candidate $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, and its consecutive count $q$
\Ensure Enhanced handover decision $\mathrm{Decision}$ and updated $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, and $q$
\State \textbf{Stage 1: RS-TOPSIS risk-sensitive ranking}
\For{$i\gets1$ \textbf{to} $3$}
 \State Append $C_i$ to closeness history and keep the latest $K_c$ periods
 \State $\sigma_i^C\gets\mathrm{std}(\text{closeness history of }C_i)$
 \State $C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}\gets C_i-0.5\sigma_i^C$
\EndFor
\State $N^*\gets\arg\max_i C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}$
\State \textbf{Stage 2: ADH adaptive-hysteresis parameter update}
\State $\sigma\gets\mathrm{std}(\text{latest five SINR values of }N_{\mathrm{cur}})$
\State $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}\gets0.03+0.05(\sigma/10)$, $T_h\gets\max(2,\lfloor4-2v/30\rfloor)$
\State \textbf{Stage 3: Robust-score dual-hysteresis decision}
\If{$N^*\ne N_{\mathrm{cand}}$}
 \State $N_{\mathrm{cand}}\gets N^*$, $q\gets0$
\EndIf
\If{$C^{\mathrm{robust}}(N^*)-C^{\mathrm{robust}}(N_{\mathrm{cur}})>\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$}
 \State $q\gets q+1$
\Else
 \State $q\gets0$
\EndIf
\If{$q\geq T_h$}
 \State $\mathrm{Decision}\gets\text{switch to }N^*$, $N_{\mathrm{cur}}\gets N^*$
\Else
 \State $\mathrm{Decision}\gets\text{keep }N_{\mathrm{cur}}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\mathrm{Decision}$, $N_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $N_{\mathrm{cand}}$, $q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Simulation Experiments and Results Analysis}\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Experimental Platform and Parameter Settings}\label{sec:platform}

To evaluate LAAVHA/ALERA in UAV heterogeneous wireless networks, a simulation and inference platform was developed using ns-3.45 \cite{ref31} and its AI extension ns3-ai \cite{ref32}. ns-3 implements the 5G/LTE/Wi-Fi topology, UAV mobility, and physical- and transport-layer measurements, while ns3-ai enables bidirectional simulation-inference communication through shared memory. Each decision period transfers a 10-step history of five indicators for three candidate networks and the mobility state to shared memory. The inference module loads the trained LAAVHA model, reads the network state, speed, altitude, and current-network identifier, and returns the target-network identifier and candidate scores. The model input comprises $3$ networks $\times10$ time steps $\times5$ indicators (150 dimensions), with each network processed independently by the stacked LSTM. Mobility input comprises speed and altitude. The model outputs a five-dimensional predicted state and dynamic weight, followed by candidate scoring through improved TOPSIS. The testing dataset uses seeds 200--449 (250 trajectories for algorithm comparison; the first 50 seeds, 200--249, are also used for ablation and QoS experiments). Each simulation lasts $50~\mathrm{s}$ with a $0.1~\mathrm{s}$ decision period, yielding 500 decisions per trajectory. Position and altitude perturbations of $30~\mathrm{m}$ and $10~\mathrm{m}$, respectively, represent flight deviations.

Before finalizing the parameters in Section~\ref{sec:laavha}, three sensitivity experiments were conducted to tune the core algorithmic parameters: the fusion coefficient $\alpha$ (balancing current and predicted states), dual-hysteresis thresholds $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $T_h$ (controlling handover triggering), and enhancement parameters $K_c$ and $\lambda$ (managing risk sensitivity). All experiments used carefully designed stress scenarios that isolate the effect of each parameter group. Figure~\ref{fig:param_sensitivity} summarizes the parameter-sensitivity results.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/fig_parameter_sensitivity_en.png}
\caption{Sensitivity analysis of the fusion coefficient, dual-hysteresis parameters, and enhancement parameters. (For (\textbf{b}) and (\textbf{c}), upper entries denote mean false-handover counts and lower entries denote mean detection~delays.)}
\label{fig:param_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\textbf{(a) Fusion coefficient $\alpha$.} To evaluate the trade-off between current measurements and predicted states, 50 stress replays involving both sustained link degradation and transient channel fluctuations were constructed. Current SINR observations captured instantaneous channel-measurement variation, while LSTM predictions exhibited short-term lag under abrupt changes. For $\alpha\in\{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$, mean false-handover counts were 1.20, 0.90, 0.08, and 1.00, respectively. Lower $\alpha$ (heavier reliance on prediction) increased sensitivity to prediction lag during rapid changes, whereas higher $\alpha$ (heavier reliance on current state) increased sensitivity to measurement noise. The optimal $\alpha=0.6$ minimized false handovers by assigning 60\% weight to current measurements and 40\% to predictions.

\textbf{(b) Dual-hysteresis parameters $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $T_h$.} Using the selected $\alpha=0.6$, parameters were evaluated on 50 trajectories containing two critical intervals: sustained degradation requiring handover, and transient fluctuations that should not trigger handover. Across $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}\in\{0.03, 0.05, 0.07\}$ and $T_h\in\{2, 3, 4\}$, all combinations achieved 100\% necessary-handover detection. The setting $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.05, T_h=3$ yielded 0.64 mean false handovers and $1.55~\mathrm{s}$ mean detection delay. The aggressive $0.03, 2$ setting reduced delay but produced 9.10 false handovers, while the conservative $0.07, 4$ setting eliminated false handovers at the cost of $2.00~\mathrm{s}$ delay. Therefore, $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.05, T_h=3$ was selected to balance stability and responsiveness.

\textbf{(c) Enhancement parameters $K_c$ and $\lambda$.} Using the tuned $\alpha=0.6$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.05, T_h=3$, the RS-TOPSIS risk-penalty parameters were evaluated on the same 50 trajectories. All combinations of $K_c\in\{3, 5, 7\}$ and $\lambda\in\{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$ achieved 100\% necessary-handover detection. At $K_c=5$, increasing $\lambda$ from 0.2 to 0.8 reduced mean false handovers from 1.90 to 0.58 but increased detection delay from $1.13~\mathrm{s}$ to $1.22~\mathrm{s}$. At $\lambda=0.5$, varying $K_c$ from 3 to 7 showed that $K_c=5$ produced the lowest false-handover count (1.08) with moderate delay ($1.17~\mathrm{s}$). Therefore, $K_c=5, \lambda=0.5$ were selected.

\subsection{Ablation-Study Validation}\label{sec:ablation}

Ablation experiments quantify the contributions of LSTM prediction and attention-based dynamic weighting to LAAVHA. LAAVHA-L increases to 5.55 (std=0.64); removing temporal prediction produces more reactive decisions and 160\% more handovers. LAAVHA-A reaches 24.25 (std=1.35); entropy weighting cannot adapt criteria to the flight phase and leads to frequent handovers. The full algorithm achieves an 11.4-fold reduction (2.13 vs. 24.25) compared to the no-attention variant. Dynamic weight adaptation is essential for handover stability.

Flight- and network-state-based weights adapt to phase-specific requirements far better than static entropy weights. Removing the LSTM increases the mean to 5.55; temporal prediction contributes an additional 61\% reduction (5.55$\rightarrow$2.13) beyond dynamic weighting alone. Together, LSTM prediction provides temporal smoothing, while attention-based weighting prevents the ping-pong effect caused by fixed weights near coverage boundaries.

\subsection{Scenario Adaptation and Enhancement Validation}\label{sec:adapt}

To evaluate ALERA under rapid coverage-boundary crossings and abrupt channel changes, a competitive scenario is constructed with network A, analogous to 5G, and network B, analogous to Wi-Fi, having closeness scores near 0.50; A is initially serving. The scenario represents two data transmission conditions: timely migration under sustained degradation and handover suppression under transient disturbances from short obstructions or multipath effects. Figure~\ref{fig:enhanced} shares the time axis across four panels, with $t=5-8~\mathrm{s}$ and $t=12-15~\mathrm{s}$ denoting the same intervals in panels a--d. Light-blue Zone 1 represents sustained degradation, where A declines and B improves, requiring a correct A-to-B handover. Light-red Zone 2 represents transient fluctuations, where sinusoidal variation and Gaussian noise are injected into B; short peaks provide no sustained mean advantage and should not trigger handover.

Panel (a) of Figure~\ref{fig:enhanced} shows raw closeness curves. The vertical axis represents the candidate's integrated score before risk penalization, with higher values indicating greater proximity to the ideal candidate. Network B maintains a persistent advantage in Zone 1, whereas its Zone 2 increase is a transient high-frequency oscillation.

Panel (b) shows handovers under the fixed low threshold ($\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}=0.03$); the vertical axis is raw closeness, and red dashed lines mark handover times and directions. Correct A$\rightarrow$B and B$\rightarrow$A handovers occur at $t=6.6~\mathrm{s}$ and $t=11.9~\mathrm{s}$, respectively, corresponding to sustained degradation and subsequent recovery. In Zone 2, transient peaks trigger A$\rightarrow$B at $t=14.8~\mathrm{s}$ and B$\rightarrow$A at $t=15.1~\mathrm{s}$, producing a false handover and subsequent ping-pong handover.

Panel (c) presents ALERA's risk-adjusted scores, with the vertical axis $C_i^{\mathrm{robust}}=C_i-\lambda\sigma_i^C$, with green dashed lines indicating handovers. ALERA retains both necessary handovers while suppressing Zone 2 fluctuations and avoiding handover.

Panel (d) shows the adaptive threshold $\Delta_{\mathrm{th}}$, representing the required closeness advantage. In Zone 2, it increases from 0.03 to approximately 0.08 and gradually decreases after fluctuations subside. ADH raises the handover barrier only under unstable conditions.

The enhancement provides two key benefits. Under sustained degradation, it completes the necessary handover from degraded network A to better network B, avoiding excessive conservatism. Under transient variation, it suppresses false handovers caused by short peaks and prevents A$\rightarrow$B$\rightarrow$A ping-pong. RS-TOPSIS reduces the ranking advantage of volatile candidates, determining which network to switch to, while ADH raises the trigger threshold during fluctuations, determining when to switch. Together, they preserve necessary handovers, reduce false handovers, and limit associated link-reestablishment overhead.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

For VHO challenges arising from wide-area flight, heterogeneous coverage, and rapid link-state changes in UAV heterogeneous wireless networks, this paper proposes LAAVHA and its enhanced version, ALERA. LAAVHA integrates stacked-LSTM short-term state prediction, attention-based dynamic attribute weighting, improved TOPSIS using current and predicted states, and dual-hysteresis decision-making. This joint use of temporal evolution, flight state, and multi-attribute service requirements reduces handovers caused by transient boundary fluctuations. LAAVHA is further enhanced with adaptive hysteresis (ADH) and risk-sensitive TOPSIS (RS-TOPSIS) to form ALERA. ADH adapts the handover threshold and confirmation window to serving-network SINR variation and flight speed, while RS-TOPSIS penalizes historically volatile candidates, improving adaptation in both handover timing and network selection. Across 250 extended 50-second trajectories, LAAVHA achieves the lowest mean handover count among 11 algorithms ($2.13$), reducing handovers by $46.0\%-94.8\%$ versus eight baselines while maintaining the highest throughput and lowest delay among evaluated algorithms. Removing LSTM prediction or dynamic attention weighting increases the mean to $5.55$ and $24.25$, respectively. The two modules play complementary roles in handover stability. Scenario-adaptation experiments demonstrate that ALERA preserves necessary handovers under sustained degradation while suppressing false handovers caused by transient variation, confirming the effectiveness of adaptive decision parameters for UAV aerial missions in heterogeneous wireless networks.
